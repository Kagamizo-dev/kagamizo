The model's errors are the first risk. The spread of a forecast high by lead and the spread of a forecast day's precipitation are fixed constants, not calibrated per city or season; a wet climate or a volatile season will have wider real errors, and the independence of days understates the variance of a monthly total. Both errors make the model overconfident, which is the direction that buys too much; the minimum edge and the caps are the only defences until a backtest measures the calibration.

The second risk is resolution: the exchange settles on a named station's observation, and the forecast is for a grid point near it. A station that reports a different high than the grid, or a settlement rule the title does not fully state (which days count, rounding, a missing report), is a loss the model never saw. Only the three question shapes are traded so that this gap stays as narrow as the rules can make it.

The third is liquidity: event contracts are thin, an ask seen when the candidates were listed may be gone by the preflight, and a position may have no bid to exit. Every proposal is a single limit order at the ask seen, for a cost the policy allows to be lost in full. Nothing in this strategy is placed by an agent; placing is the owner's step, and only once the stage gate has been passed with the evidence the proofs require.
